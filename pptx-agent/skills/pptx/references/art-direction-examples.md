# Examples of design decisions, not templates

Read a relevant example when turning content into a visual expression is difficult.
The examples explain operations and their limits. They do not prescribe chapter
structures, artist choices, layouts or a picture count for unrelated tasks.

## Scientific mechanism: parallel exploration versus sequential refinement

Audience question: what changes when the budget increases width rather than depth?
Available facts might describe several candidates sharing an evidence pool versus
one result/history passed into a later refinement. Use the paper's actual mechanism,
conditions and notation; this example supplies no benchmark claim or invented score.

An initial page might place both descriptions inside identical chains of boxes.
The text can be correct while the visual fails to expose the difference.
A possible design makes shared evidence visible, with parallel branches arriving
at several candidates. The refinement view instead follows one persistent object
through successive states, showing what information carries forward. Match the
encoding of genuinely shared elements. Keep conditions and selector rules near the
part they qualify rather than hiding them in a generic footer.

Another valid design could show aligned state snapshots. Choose based on which
mechanism and speaking sequence need to be inspected. The key is the visible
difference, not a mandatory fork diagram. In a live explanation, retain orientation
and reveal the contrasting relationships in semantic groups.

Rendered check: can the viewer trace independent alternatives versus accumulated
state without reading the entire paragraph? Are any new arrows asserting a relation
that the paper does not support? Do all necessary labels remain visible at reading size?

## Research evidence: a full table with a clear argument

Audience question: which observations support the stated result, and where are its
limits? Means, uncertainty, baselines, configurations and units may all be necessary.
A large claim above equally emphasized cells still leaves the viewer to find its proof.

A possible design keeps the complete comparable table, uses a quiet consistent
baseline treatment, and draws attention to the specific pairs discussed in the
conclusion. Put the correct increment/unit near those pairs. Native click groups
can guide the live sequence while the final view retains all required evidence.
A different dataset may be better served by aligned plots or small multiples.
Choose the representation from the comparison and actual data, preserving honest
scales and uncertainty. Do not force every table into a chart.

Rendered check: can the audience find the claim's evidence, compare like with like,
and see the qualification? Is the emphasis earned, and does it remain coherent
with related result pages? Avoid a different highlight system on every page.

## A historical text: connect exact words with authentic evidence

Audience question: what argument is made, in what situation, and what supports the
interpretation? The exact passage and historical context matter more than atmosphere.
Use the verified wording and authentic document/photo when suitable material exists.

A possible page gives a short sourced passage strong typographic presence, places
an authentic document view nearby, and enlarges a verified relevant detail. Short
native annotations connect the text, situation and interpretation. Another page
may use a designed quotation alone when no useful image is available. The reading
path and hierarchy can make a text-led page deliberate and complete.

A photographic or editorial reference may inform crop, tonal areas or the relation
between a large quotation and small context. Transfer that relationship using this
subject's actual evidence. Do not generate an archival photograph, invent a marked
passage or imply a quote appears in the shown document without checking it.

Rendered check: does the audience know which words are original, which are analysis,
and why the image is present? Can they read the necessary passage and see its
connection? Keep context; a dramatic crop cannot erase the evidence's identity.

## Product or proposed experience: make the output visible

Audience question: what can a person actually do or experience, and what changes?
A page listing benefits may leave the useful result invisible.

For a real product, show an inspected actual screen or output at useful scale,
with editable annotations pointing to the relevant behavior. For a hypothetical
proposal, an original concept illustration may show a setting or intended effect;
label it illustrative and keep analytical relationships and quantities native.
Use available sources/generation according to the actual brief and tool capabilities.

A relevant photographic/film reference could inform foreground/background, selective
focus or a quiet area for labels. The picture should participate in the explanation;
placing a tiny image beside unchanged paragraphs rarely changes the reading task.
Another page may need a native permissions or cost diagram instead of imagery.

Rendered check: can the viewer see the subject/output and distinguish real evidence
from a proposed scene? Does the image provide enough useful detail for its labels,
and does the page still belong to the surrounding technical explanation?

## Continuity: overview, detail and evidence

A sequence may introduce a system, enlarge one component to expose its mechanism,
then return to stable quantitative comparison. Reuse the component's identity,
color meaning and annotation grammar while changing scale and density for each job.
A shared path, baseline or object can carry the transition. There is no need to
redraw every page as an identical container grid or to introduce a different visual
metaphor for every topic. Other narratives may call for another sequence entirely.

For example, a mechanism might separate detecting a discrepancy from correcting
it. A following case that lists file names and a success score can hide that
distinction. Select a source-supported discrepancy or rule application, expose its
consequence, and retain the two operations' identity through labels, alignment or
another useful relationship. Keep the case focused instead of copying the entire
overview again. If the source supplies only a rule, show that exact evidence; use a
clearly labeled teaching instance only where allowed, never a fabricated case result.
Rendered check: can the audience point to the instance of each necessary operation
and see the detail supporting the result? Source notes can document provenance,
but should not be the only place this essential mapping exists.

When studying a reference series, examine both what repeats and what changes.
Borrow a useful continuity principle, test it on the current pages and retain its
concrete application. A list of palette values or artist names does not supply
that continuity by itself.

## When the first idea should be abandoned

A researcher may suggest a chronological strip for a report with several case
studies. The actual material later shows that the cases are independent and their
useful evidence is large photographs with different details. A strip of tiny
photos and long captions implies an unsupported sequence and hides those details.
Adding connectors or shrinking captions will not resolve that mismatch.

The designer can instead compose the first case around an inspectable photograph
and native evidence annotations, then use a stable label/color grammar to relate
the other cases. Update the provisional direction and affected asset/page notes;
keep dates, identities, evidence limits and protected template elements. Another
subject might genuinely need the chronological strip: the decision follows the
actual relationship and material, not a universal preference for photographs.

When authoring in order, the first case render can expose this early. Later section
previews may reveal a different mismatch. Change only the affected treatment and
reuse successful material; avoid either repeating a failed scheme or restarting
all completed pages whenever an alternative becomes imaginable.
