# Poster thinking

A deck is a sequence; a page is a poster. Design the deck as a deck designer — order,
rhythm, one visual grammar — and design each key page as if you were a poster designer
standing in front of a wall: one image, one line, one move, no polite default.

Read this with [art direction](art-direction.md). It replaces neither the deck's
consistency nor the page's job; it raises what a single page can do.

## Placed versus made

Look at how a picture sits on the page. Two states:

- **Placed**: the picture is an object in a white field, four rectangular edges, a gap
  around it, the type beside it in another box. Common, safe, and the reason pages read
  as documents.
- **Made**: the picture bleeds to two or more edges, shares a colour or a material with
  the type field, and meets the type along a boundary that has a shape. The page is one
  surface, not two boxes.

A made page does not need more elements. It usually needs fewer, larger and better
related. A rectangle next to a text block is placement; a full-height picture, a shaped
seam and a colour taken from the picture is a page.

## Look at the evidence first

Before borrowing anything, read the actual image you are thinking of using — every page,
not only the cover. Answer seven questions in your own words:

1. Where does the eye land first, and what is that object doing?
2. Where does it go next, and what carries it there?
3. What has been enlarged, and what shrunk, against its real importance?
4. What has been left out? (A good picture is usually defined by what it removed.)
5. What is the light–dark structure once content is ignored?
6. What are the edges and the empty areas doing?
7. If you could take one decision from it, which one?

Then decide, concretely: **keep three things, replace three things**. "Keep the subject,
the vertical rising line and the dark frame; replace the ground, the photographic
texture and the type register." That sentence is the design.

## A menu, not a checklist

These are directions people have taken. They are here to widen the search, not to be
chosen and applied. Derive the move from the material in front of you; when a listed
move is the right one, push it further than the example.

**With the picture**
- full bleed to two, three or four edges; crop into a detail until the subject becomes a shape
- make the picture the ground and set type over it (dim, mist or scrim the area under the type)
- turn it into a window, a door, a hole, a frame, an aperture
- repeat, mirror, offset or tile the same image; slice it into bands with a thin seam
- pair two images at incompatible scales (a detail beside its whole)
- let half the picture stay photographic and the other half become line, ink, silhouette or flat colour

**At the boundary**
- a curve, an arc, an S, a torn edge, a brush edge, a cut corner, a diagonal
- trace the seam with a hairline, or set a darker shape behind it for a sticker or paper-cut layer
- make the seam follow the shape already inside the picture (a trunk, a ridge, a hand)
- let the picture's darkest area run into the type field so the two share a material

**Type**
- one word or phrase at display size, broken into short lines, ragged right
- type as image: a character at half-page height, cropped by the edge
- very light weight with wide tracking, or a display face with real character
- two registers only (a display line and a small supporting line), never four
- vertical setting, or type following a curve or a slant
- type overlapping the subject so the two belong to the same object

**Colour**
- sample the type field from the picture (its shadow, its stone, its water)
- one saturated accent, everything else two or three steps of one hue
- a duotone or overprint feel
- a single flat colour field that exists nowhere in the picture and yet holds it
- deliberate near-monochrome with one warm or one cold note

**Space**
- luxury of emptiness: subject at 20% of the page
- extreme asymmetry, one heavy side and one quiet side
- break the grid on purpose; keep one alignment line to hold the page
- density as a choice: a crowded field that reads as texture, one calm area

**Material**
- paper, cloth, stone, metal, plaster as ground
- grain, noise, photocopy, letterpress, risograph
- collage, torn paper, tape, stamp, stencil
- a thin rule or a registration mark borrowed from print

**Transformations** (when the source picture is ordinary)
- change the medium: photograph → ink wash, woodcut, pencil, gouache, flat vector
- redraw a texture as a sign: ripples become concentric lines, foliage becomes dots, light becomes a gradient
- reduce to two or three tones and one accent
- extract the silhouette and discard everything else
- recrop or re-view: from above, from below, closer, through something
- rebuild the scene with the subject kept and everything else invented
- push one contrast to its limit (size, light, density, saturation) and keep the rest quiet
- add a typographic layer that turns an image into a printed object

If none of these fits, invent one. The menu exists so that "I could not think of
anything" stops being true.

## Three worked examples

**A plain swan photo → a plate.** The photograph is ordinary: a black swan on green
water, soft light, no drama. Kept: the bird's silhouette, its direction, the quiet. Replaced:
the water becomes paper, the real ripples become a few drawn concentric lines, the
feather detail becomes flat ink, the palette drops to black, grey-green and one red bill.
Added: a serif italic title line and a small supporting line. The result is no longer a
photo of a swan; it is a print about stillness. Nothing was copied from another design —
the move came from asking what the picture was *about*.

**A tree through a hollow trunk → a poster.** The original page was a white field with
type on the left and a shadowed photo on the right: placed. Kept: the photograph, the
vertical rise, the dark frame of the trunk. Replaced: the white ground with a dark brown
sampled from the trunk, the rectangular edge with an S-shaped seam traced by a hairline,
the single-line title with three short ragged lines in a rounded display face. The photo
now runs to three edges and its darkness merges into the type field. One move — the seam —
does most of the work.

**A road through palms, seen from above → a quiet cover.** Kept: the diagonal, the road
as the only clear line, the mass of green. Replaced: the busy foliage becomes a soft wash
by misting everything except the road; the ground becomes a large empty field; the title
becomes a very light, widely tracked line at display size with a small Chinese line below.
One idea: a single path stays clear while everything else recedes.

## Inside the deck's grammar

A poster page is a solo, not a different band. Before page 1 the deck fixes:

- one accent colour and its role
- one type scale (sizes and weights, two or three levels)
- one boundary language for the deck (curves, tears, straight cuts — pick one family)
- one picture treatment (misted, full colour, duotone, cropped hard)

Each key page may take a different move inside that grammar. Materials and type should
look like the same hand made the whole deck.

## A requested style is the page's style, not the pictures

When the brief names a style (hand-drawn, pixel, collage, risograph, watercolour), it
applies to the whole page system, not only to the illustrations. Hand-drawn pictures on a
corporate page — white field, navy cards, teal headings, thin office rules — read as a
report with drawings pasted in. Carry the style into the native layer: a paper or tinted
ground, rules and marks drawn by hand (freeform paths, slightly uneven), a handwriting or
brush face for notes (KaiTi), captions set like a sketchbook; for pixel style, a visible
grid, stepped edges, a bitmap-like display face and a limited palette. Keep text editable
and readable; change the material, not the legibility.

## The rule that keeps it from becoming a template

For every key page, name the page's move in one sentence (the page plan's `poster_move`).
At least one decision on the page must not be copied from any reference: invented from the
material, or a borrowed move pushed further than its source. If the page could have been
produced by applying a menu item unchanged, it is a template again — go back to the
picture and ask what it is about.

## Check before saving

- Can you name the page's move in one sentence?
- Is the picture material, or decoration placed on a field?
- Is the seam shaped, or a straight cut?
- Do the type and the picture share a world (colour, weight, position, depth)?
- At stamp size, is there one thing that holds the page?
- Would this page still be recognised as a poster if the deck's other pages disappeared?
